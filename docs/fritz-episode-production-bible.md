# Fritz Academy Episode Production Bible

Status: CANONICAL production contract for all 108 episodes.

## Student experience
Fritz Academy is a game/adventure series with a rigorous English curriculum underneath it. The child should feel that they are helping the Fritz Academy dogs solve problems, explore, build, find clues, play games and complete missions. The interface and writing must not feel like a classroom worksheet with a story pasted on top.

Target runtime: approximately 20 minutes per episode, including story/adventure, interaction, mini-games, Music Box/song time, reader/listening play, builder/reward and transitions. Individual segments should generally change mode every 2–4 minutes.

## Character roles
- Captain Fritz — mentor. Warm, experienced guide. He gives the group room to think and solve problems rather than supplying every answer.
- Bash — the real leader. Calm, capable, observant and dependable. Bash naturally organizes the group and makes good decisions without constantly announcing that he is the leader.
- Tony — thinks he is in charge. Tony confidently appoints himself leader and announces plans. His certainty is a recurring source of comedy, but he is a valued member of the group rather than a fool.
- Nola — the worrier. She notices risks, anticipates what might go wrong and asks questions. Her concern naturally creates prediction, reassurance, question-language and problem-solving opportunities.
- Rascal — the troublemaker. Curious, impulsive and playful. His choices frequently trigger the episode problem. He is mischievous rather than cruel or unsafe.
- Bear — semi-troublemaker. Bash's brother. Bear can be tempted into Rascal's schemes, but his younger yet much larger brother Bash regularly catches/stops him before the scheme goes too far.

Character roles must remain recognizable without becoming repetitive caricatures. Different characters can succeed, make mistakes, discover clues and help one another.

## Episode engine
Every episode needs a dramatic/game reason for the target language to matter.

1. COLD OPEN / HOOK (1–2 min)
   Something is happening before anyone explains the learning target. A missing object, strange clue, unfinished build, mixed-up sign, Rascal scheme, Academy event, challenge, map, mystery or mission creates immediate purpose.

2. CHARACTER PROBLEM + PLAYER CHOICE (2–3 min)
   The cast reacts in character. Tony declares a plan, Nola identifies a concern, Rascal/Bear complicate matters, Bash focuses the team, Captain Fritz mentors. The player makes a meaningful choice or identifies a clue.

3. DISCOVERY PLAY / LANGUAGE IN ACTION (2–3 min)
   Target letters, sounds, words, questions or phrases appear because they help solve the problem. Avoid announcing a lesson objective to the child.

4. MINI-GAME (2–3 min)
   A short replayable interaction: find, sort, match, chase, collect, choose, sequence, build, listen-and-move, clue hunt, sound hunt, path selection, memory, timing or another age-appropriate mechanic. Correct language use advances the adventure.

5. STORY TURN + MUSIC BOX / SONG (2–3 min)
   The situation changes or a new clue appears. A reusable curriculum song can be unlocked/played when natural. Songs reinforce rather than interrupt the adventure.

6. READER / LISTENING MISSION (2–3 min)
   Controlled text, signs, notes, map clues, messages or a short reader provide information the team actually needs. Reading is therefore a game action, not a detached assignment.

7. FINAL GAME / BUILDER PAYOFF (2–3 min)
   The player uses accumulated clues/language to solve the episode problem and earns/places a Builder piece or advances an Academy build.

8. TAG / NEXT-EPISODE THREAD (30–60 sec)
   Character comedy, consequence or discovery gives the episode closure and can seed the next adventure.

Timing is a production target, not a rigid stopwatch. The total experience should land around 20 minutes for the intended learner, including response time and replayable interaction.

## Curriculum invisibility rule
Internal metadata can name objectives. Student-facing dialogue should normally not say things such as `Today we will learn A, B and C` or `Now answer the comprehension questions.` Instead: `These blocks have marks on them. Which one matches the sign?` The same learning occurs through the mission.

## First nine episode arc
The first nine episodes form one connected Alphabet Foundation adventure. A–Z and question comprehension are the curriculum spine, while the visible story spine is the group building/restoring/exploring Fritz Academy together.

Episode 1 A/B/C + WHAT: The Welcome Garden Mix-Up. Rascal and Bear have moved the first Builder crates; Tony announces he can fix everything without the labels. Bash realizes the A/B/C crate marks are the key. The player joins the team and uses WHAT questions to identify objects/clues.

Episode 2 D/E/F + WHO: Who Took the Flag? A harmless missing Academy flag creates an identity/clue hunt. Tony starts investigating everyone. Nola worries the welcome event cannot start. WHO language and D/E/F clues reveal what happened.

Episode 3 G/H/I + WHAT/WHO + yes/no: The Mystery Gate. The Welcome Garden gate will not open because its G/H/I markers have been mixed up. Rascal insists he did not touch them; Bear is suspiciously unconvincing. Player uses yes/no and who/what clues to repair it. Week 1 build payoff.

Episode 4 J/K/L + WHERE: The Lost Key Trail. A Builder key is missing somewhere in the Academy grounds. WHERE and position language drive a map/search mini-game.

Episode 5 M/N/O + HOW/HOW MANY: The Bridge With Missing Pieces. The dogs need to cross/repair a safe Academy garden bridge. Player counts pieces and follows HOW/HOW MANY clues. Tony's proposed shortcut is rejected by Bash.

Episode 6 P/Q/R + WHICH: The Three Paths. Conflicting signs point toward different Academy areas. The player uses WHICH choices, letter/sound clues and visual attributes to select the correct route. Week 2 build payoff.

Episode 7 S/T/U + WHEN: The Music Box Countdown. The group must get several Academy preparations done in the correct order before the Music Box event. WHEN, now/later and sequence language drive the mission.

Episode 8 V/W/X + WHY: The Strange Tracks. Odd tracks/clues appear near an unfinished build. Nola imagines several explanations. WHY/Because language is grounded in visible cause/effect evidence. The reveal remains funny and safe.

Episode 9 Y/Z + A–Z cumulative questions: The Alphabet Builder Challenge. A final chain of clues across the Academy requires A–Z retrieval plus who/what/where/when/why/how/how many/which/yes-no comprehension. Solving it completes the Alphabet Foundation build and unlocks the next adventure arc focused more intensely on decoding.

## Mini-game rule
Every episode needs at least one genuine interactive mini-game plus interactive choices elsewhere. A multiple-choice quiz by itself does not satisfy this requirement. Mini-games should be reusable engine patterns with new content/data rather than 108 bespoke engines.

## Song rule
Songs are recurring Music Box assets and curriculum anchors. The Alphabet Song can recur throughout Episodes 1–9 and later for review. Other songs unlock when their language becomes useful. Do not force a unique song into every episode.

## Reader rule
A reader or reading interaction must affect the adventure: reveal a clue, identify a place/object, help a character, choose a path, repair/build something or solve the mission. Early text is controlled and increasingly decodable.

## Continuity rule
Episodes remember the world. Builder areas visibly progress. Character relationships persist. A joke, object, discovery or consequence can carry into later episodes. The course should feel like a season of an educational adventure game, not 108 disconnected lessons.

## Production acceptance
An episode is not production-complete unless:
- the character behavior matches canon;
- the adventure has a hook, problem, turn and payoff;
- curriculum is embedded in gameplay;
- target play time is approximately 20 minutes;
- at least one mini-game is present;
- song/Music Box time is included when appropriate;
- reading/listening advances the mission;
- builder/progression payoff works;
- referenced visual/audio assets are valid or explicitly queued for production;
- unlock/save behavior passes runtime QA;
- the episode is enjoyable even when the learner does not know the internal curriculum objective.
